# Evidence-Based Blueprint and Bilingual Research Dossier for a Grade 4 Learning App in Baden-Württemberg

Building an effective learning application for fourth-grade pupils (ages 9–10, *Klassenstufe 4*) in Baden-Württemberg requires unifying international cognitive and developmental science (predominantly English-language research) with the curricular, psychometric, media-ecological, legal, and school-IT realities of Germany and Baden-Württemberg. The foundational pedagogical conclusion of this dossier is that a modern primary-school app must reject "learning style" classification (*Lerntypen* such as VARK or Frederic Vester's typology), which lacks empirical support for improving achievement and risks locking nine-year-olds into fixed ability beliefs [cite: 1, 2, 3, 4]. Instead, the app's instructional engine should operationalize six evidence-based cognitive strategies—anchored by guided retrieval practice, spaced repetition, and dual coding—embedded within a Self-Regulated Learning (*Selbstreguliertes Lernen*, SRL) cycle [cite: 5, 6, 7].

To achieve relevance and adoption in Baden-Württemberg, product architecture must map directly onto the updated **Bildungsplan Grundschule (V2, 2024)** and the **NAVi 4 BW / Kompass 4** secondary-school transition framework, which links Gymnasium eligibility to parental choice combined with either the class conference's holistic evaluation (*pädagogische Gesamtwürdigung*, including *Lern- und Arbeitsverhalten*) or reaching the extended competence tier (*Niveau E*) in central fourth-grade diagnostics [cite: 8, 9, 10, 11]. Because German fourth graders face rapidly expanding digital exposure—daily internet use among online 8- to 9-year-olds rose from 23% in 2022 to 40% in 2024 [cite: 12]—while 55% of parents of smartphone-owning children apply neither technical limits nor usage conversations [cite: 12, 13], an app entering this market should differentiate itself through autonomy-supportive, zero-dark-pattern UX [cite: 14, 15].

Commercial and institutional viability in Germany further depends on strict legal and infrastructural compliance. Under Article 8 DSGVO (GDPR), Germany sets the digital age of consent at 16 years, meaning direct-to-consumer apps for 9- to 10-year-olds require verifiable parental consent, child-intelligible privacy notices, and strict data minimization without profiling [cite: 16, 17]. For public-school deployment in Baden-Württemberg, the app must avoid third-party telemetry and mandatory personal student accounts, align with **SCHULE@BW** Single Sign-On and **LfDI BW** privacy standards, and support **Apple School Manager (ASM)** Volume Purchase Program (VPP) licensing for shared tablet carts (*Geteiltes iPad*) managed via local **paed.ML / Relution** or **Jamf School** servers [cite: 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24].

## 1. Pedagogical DNA: Debunking the Learning-Styles Myth and Operationalizing Cognitive Science

### 1.1 English and German Evidence Against the Matching Hypothesis (*Lerntypenmythos*)

A common mistake in children's educational software is opening with a diagnostic quiz that categorizes pupils into sensory modalities—such as Fleming's **VARK** (Visual, Aural, Read/Write, Kinesthetic) or **Frederic Vester's (1975)** German typology (*visuell*, *auditiv*, *haptisch*, *intellektuell*)—and subsequently filtering instruction to match that label [cite: 2, 4, 25, 26]. Across both English-language cognitive psychology and German empirical educational research (*Empirische Bildungsforschung*), the meshing or matching hypothesis (*Passungshypothese*) lacks adequate empirical support as a general instructional principle [cite: 1, 2, 3, 25].

Syntheses by Coffield et al. (2004), Pashler et al. (2008), Rohrer and Pashler (2012), Willingham et al. (2015), and Newton and Salvi (2020) establish that showing a learner prefers or enjoys a format does not demonstrate improved learning when instruction is matched to that preference [cite: 1, 2, 25]. Validating the matching hypothesis requires a statistically significant **crossover interaction**, where one style group performs best with Instruction A and another performs best with Instruction B [cite: 2, 27]. Across four matching meta-analyses reviewed in a 2025 *Educational Psychology Review* synthesis, the overall matching effect remained close to zero [cite: 25]. A separate systematic meta-analysis of 21 modality-matching studies (101 effect sizes; 1,712 participants) using robust variance estimation reported a small aggregate effect (g = 0.31, SE = 0.12, 95% CI [0.05, 0.57], p = 0.02), yet only 26% of learning outcome measures exhibited the required crossover interaction (and 25% across 12 additional studies lacking full statistical details); citing low study quality alongside implementation costs, the authors concluded that the benefits are too small and infrequent to justify widespread adoption [cite: 27]. In an observational elementary-school study of 109 pupils (48 visual [44.04%], 40 auditory [36.70%], and 21 kinesthetic [19.27%]; grand mean performance 80.95), ANOVA testing likewise found no statistically significant relationship between learning-style preference and academic achievement [cite: 28]. *(Note on source access: Verbatim instructions, scoring keys, and the 16 items from the vendor-hosted "VARK Questionnaire for Younger People" could not be extracted due to site access restrictions [cite: 29]; however, the empirical failure of VARK-based matching renders those items unnecessary for product design.)*

Despite scientific refutation, the neuromyth remains deeply entrenched among educators and parents. In a higher-education survey, 64% of academics reported attempting to accommodate learning styles and 46% believed doing so benefited learning even after reviewing the counter-evidence, with roughly one-third stating they would continue using them [cite: 1]. In German-speaking teacher training, Krammer et al. (2019) reported agreement rates exceeding 97% among pre-service teachers (*Lehramtsstudierende*), echoing findings by Dekker et al. (2012) and Grospietsch and Mayer (2018) [cite: 3]. Research by Nancekivell, Shah, and Gelman (2020) clarifies why this belief poses developmental risks: 66% of surveyed respondents fell into an "essentializer" cluster (mean 3.71 vs. 3.00 for non-essentializers, p < 0.001) that treats learning styles as innate, biologically fixed traits [cite: 2, 30]. Commentators caution that labeling a nine-year-old as "visual" or "kinesthetic" may encourage fixed-mindset attributions—leading a child who struggles with reading or symbolic math to assume a permanent mismatch rather than applying strategy and practice—while diverting finite time and budgets away from effective instruction [cite: 2, 3]. Encouragingly, German intervention research demonstrates that conceptual-change podcasts explicitly contrasting the myth against evidence in everyday language produce large, durable reductions in belief (d = 1.95 short-term; d = 1.77 long-term, p < 0.001) [cite: 3], offering a clear model for parent-facing onboarding inside the app.

### 1.2 Six Cognitive Science Strategies and Developmental Adaptation for Ages 9–10

Rather than tailoring delivery to supposed learner "types," instructional design should fit the presentation modality to the subject matter and deploy the six cognitive strategies synthesized by Weinstein et al. (2018), Nebel (2020), and UK Ofsted (2019) [cite: 6, 7, 31].

| Strategy (English / German) | Cognitive Mechanism | Evidence Base & Caveats | Grade 4 (Age 9–10) App Implementation Rules |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Retrieval Practice** (*Aktiver Abruf / Testeffekt*) | Forces active reconstruction from long-term memory rather than passive rereading [cite: 6, 32, 33]. | Strong retention gains across educational psychology reviews, though higher mental effort reduces spontaneous use [cite: 6, 33]. Elementary-school studies emphasize the **need for guided retrieval** [cite: 7]. | Avoid unguided blank-page recall; use scaffolded prompts (cloze tasks, sentence ordering, error-spotting, low-stakes mini-quizzes) with immediate corrective feedback [cite: 7, 32, 34]. |
| **Spaced Repetition** (*Verteiltes Lernen*) | Distributes practice across expanding time intervals rather than massed cramming [cite: 6, 32, 35]. | Kim and Webb's (2022) meta-analysis and Murphy's (2024) primary EFL review show durable vocabulary retention when paired with retrieval and feedback, though learners often misperceive massed practice as easier [cite: 36]. | Embed adaptive review intervals (e.g., Day 1, 3, 7, 14 adjusted by item difficulty) [cite: 35]; keep daily review bursts concise so 9-year-olds stay engaged without fatigue [cite: 37]. |
| **Dual Coding** (*Duale Kodierung*) | Combines verbal and non-verbal (pictorial/spatial) stimuli simultaneously (Paivio) so working memory forms dual cues [cite: 6, 38]. | Enhances encoding and recall when visuals clarify structure; impairs learning if dense on-screen text is narrated simultaneously, causing cognitive overload [cite: 38]. | Pair concise labels or audio with meaningful visuals (number lines, *Würfelgebäude* plans, *Satzglieder* color-blocks, science schematics)—never decorative clipart [cite: 32, 38, 39, 40]. |
| **Interleaved Practice** (*Verschachteltes Üben*) | Alternates between related problem types or topics within a session [cite: 6, 33]. | Deepens discrimination between problem structures and strengthens transfer compared with single-topic blocking [cite: 6]. | After initial blocked mastery of a single rule (e.g., *e/ä* vs. *g/k* or multiplication vs. division word problems), mix task types in review rounds [cite: 6, 39]. |
| **Elaboration** (*Elaboratives Verknüpfen*) | Associates new material with prior knowledge via "how" and "why" reasoning [cite: 6, 31]. | Supports deeper semantic integration; cognitive syntheses note stronger durable encoding than passive highlighting [cite: 6, 31]. | Prompt pupils to cite text evidence (*Begründungen im Text finden*) or explain *why* a spelling rule (*Verlängern*, *Ableiten*) applies [cite: 39, 41]. |
| **Concrete Examples** (*Konkrete & Lösungsbeispiele*) | Anchors abstract principles in concrete scenarios and step-by-step worked examples [cite: 6, 32]. | Reduces extraneous working-memory load for novices tackling unfamiliar procedures [cite: 6, 32]. | Present a clear **worked example** (*Musterbeispiel*) before asking fourth graders to build combinatorics tree diagrams or multi-step *Sachaufgaben* [cite: 32, 39]. |

### 1.3 Self-Regulated Learning (*Selbstreguliertes Lernen*, SRL) in Grade 4

Because retrieval, spacing, and interleaving feel harder than passive rereading ("desirable difficulties"), children rarely sustain them without metacognitive and motivational scaffolding [cite: 6, 35, 42]. **Zimmerman's cyclical model** structures SRL into three phases: forethought (task analysis, goal setting, strategic planning, self-efficacy), performance (strategy execution, time and environment structuring, metacognitive self-monitoring), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, causal attribution, adaptive emotional reactions feeding the next cycle) [cite: 5, 43]. Complementarily, **Boekaerts' three-layer model** distinguishes cognitive regulation of information processing, metacognitive regulation of the learning process, and motivational regulation of the self [cite: 44, 45].

German educational research demonstrates that SRL can be cultivated well before secondary school. Meta-analyses and classroom interventions by **Dignath, Büttner, and Langfeldt (2008)** and **Stoeger and Ziegler (2008a, 2010)** indicate that upper-elementary pupils (Grades 3–4, ages 8–10) can successfully acquire cognitive and metacognitive SRL strategies [cite: 5, 46, 47]. Baseline German findings further reveal that even high-intelligence fourth graders without explicit SRL instruction do not spontaneously show stronger SRL preference than average-intelligence peers, meaning structured SRL support benefits the entire cohort [cite: 5, 47]. However, developmental limits must be respected: in a four-week primary spelling study using a seven-step cyclical model, roughly two-thirds of pupils learned to set realistic weekly test goals, whereas one-third continued to struggle regardless of grade level [cite: 5]. Following **Zimmerman and Kitsantas (2002)**, self-regulation develops sequentially from observing social models, through guided emulation and externalized self-control, to independent adaptive self-regulation [cite: 43].

For app design, **Hasselhorn's (1996)** distinction between **production deficits** (*Produktionsdefizite*—knowing a strategy but failing to initiate it spontaneously) and **utilization deficits** (*Nutzungsdefizite*—applying a strategy without yet achieving measurable gains) is decisive [cite: 42]. The app must act as a fading scaffold that initially models goal-setting, prompts strategy choice during tasks, and gradually transfers control to the learner [cite: 5, 43, 48].

## 2. Baden-Württemberg Institutional, Curricular, and Transition Context

### 2.1 NAVi 4 BW, Kompass 4, and the 2026/27 Grade 4 Transition

In Baden-Württemberg, fourth grade marks the decisive transition (*Übergang Grundschule – weiterführende Schule*), governed since the 2024/2025 school year by the **NAVi 4 BW** (*Neues Aufnahmeverfahren in Klasse 4*) reform and the mandatory statewide competence assessment **Kompass 4**, developed by the *Institut für Bildungsanalysen Baden-Württemberg* (IBBW) [cite: 8, 9]. Under this framework, parental choice (*Elternwille*) remains decisive for admission to *Haupt-/Werkrealschule*, *Realschule*, and *Gemeinschaftsschule* [cite: 9]. Access to the **Gymnasium**, however, requires both parental choice and either a Gymnasium recommendation from the class conference (*pädagogische Gesamtwürdigung der Klassenkonferenz*) or an appropriate competence result in **Kompass 4**; if neither criterion is met, parents may register their child for a centralized **Potenzialtest** conducted at a Gymnasium [cite: 8, 9, 49].

Following first-year implementation feedback, the *Kultusministerium Baden-Württemberg* revised Kompass 4 for 2025/26 to shift testing from Tuesday/Wednesday to Wednesday/Thursday (relieving school administrative workload), refine task difficulty distribution, complexity, time fit, and linguistic fairness (*Sprachsensibilität*), make partial-credit scoring more visible on parent reports, and open voluntary participation to state-approved private and Waldorf schools [cite: 50]. Officially, Kompass 4 consists of a subject-specific section in **Deutsch** (*Leseverständnis*, *Rechtschreiben*, *Sprachgebrauch*) and **Mathematik** (*Zahlen und Operationen*, *Raum und Form*, *Größen und Messen*, *Daten und Zufall*), graded by subject teachers using uniform statewide rubrics, alongside a non-graded **overarching section (*überfachlicher Teil*)** capturing teacher assessments of **learning and work behavior (*Lern- und Arbeitsverhalten*)** [cite: 9, 51]. Those overarching ratings—covering concentration, persistence, self-reliance, handling setbacks, cooperation, self-expectations, and presentation—do not alter the Kompass 4 score itself, but directly inform the parent-teacher counseling conferences and the class conference's recommendation [cite: 9, 52, 53].

While official IBBW and Ministry brochures define the structure and criteria above [cite: 9, 50, 51], specific operational benchmarks, historical distributions, and 2026/27 calendar dates are documented in Baden-Württemberg transition portals and commercial test-prep guides (such as `abschluss-bw.de`, `testhelden.com`, and `anwalt.de`) [cite: 39, 49, 54]. According to these transition guides, Kompass 4 comprises two 45-minute morning exams (starting between 8:30 and 9:30 AM) spanning 50% basic (*grundlegendes Niveau G*), 25% intermediate (*mittleres Niveau M*), and 25% extended (*erweitertes Niveau E*) items, with results reported in tiers G, M, or E rather than report-card grades [cite: 39, 49]. Reported 2025/26 thresholds required an average of at least 50% across both subjects (minimum 25% per subject) for **Niveau M** and at least 75% average (minimum 50% per subject) for **Niveau E**, whereas class-conference recommendations typically reference a Deutsch/Mathematik grade average of 2.5 or better (no single grade worse than 3.0) for Gymnasium and 3.0 or better (no worse than 3.5) for Realschule [cite: 39, 49, 53, 54]. A published summary of prior-year (2025) statewide outcomes reports that 32% of pupils reached Niveau G, 43% Niveau M, and 25% Niveau E [cite: 39]. For the current **2026/27** cohort, transition guides list the Kompass 4 test dates as **November 18, 2026** (Deutsch) and **November 19, 2026** (Mathematik)—recently completed—followed by mandatory parent counseling meetings from **December 2026 through January 29, 2027**, issuance of half-year reports and recommendations by **February 5, 2027**, Potenzialtest registration on **February 6 and 8–10, 2027**, and the Potenzialtest itself on **February 23, 2027** [cite: 49].

## 3. Psychometric and Diagnostic Design for 9–10-Year-Olds

### 3.1 Validated German Instruments vs. Adolescent/Adult Inventories

If the app tracks motivational or socio-emotional dimensions alongside academic mastery, designers should avoid importing unmodified adult or adolescent instruments such as the **MSLQ** (81 items across 6 motivation and 9 strategy subscales on a 7-point Likert scale) or the German **LIST** inventory [cite: 42, 69, 70]. German primary-school diagnostics rely on age-validated benchmarks led by the **FEESS 3–4** (*Fragebogen zur Erfassung emotionaler und sozialer Schulerfahrungen von Grundschulkindern dritter und vierter Klassen*, Rauer & Schuck, 2003/2004/2021) [cite: 71, 72]. The FEESS 3–4 presents 90 statements on a 4-point scale (0 = *stimmt gar nicht* to 3 = *stimmt genau*), divided into **SIKS** (37 items covering academic self-concept, social integration, classroom climate, and feeling accepted by teachers—showing internal consistencies around α = .86 in Grade 3/4 samples) and **SALGA** (school attitude, joy of learning, readiness to exert effort) [cite: 71, 73, 74, 75]. In the parallel FEESS 1–2 validation, internal consistencies ranged from α = .63 to .94, one-year retest reliabilities varied between r_tt = .34 and .53 (reflecting real developmental sensitivity), and ability self-concept correlated up to r = .40 with achievement tests and r = .41 with teacher ratings [cite: 74]. Complementary validated German tools include **SESSKO** (Schöne et al., 2012, Grades 3–10) for academic self-concept and the teacher-rated **LSL** (*Lehrereinschätzliste für Sozial- und Lernverhalten*, Petermann & Petermann, 2013) [cite: 72, 76].

### 3.2 Measurement Biases and Hybrid Telemetry Architecture

Deploying lengthy self-report questionnaires inside a children's app introduces three well-documented validity failures. First, **acquiescence bias (*Ja-Sage-Tendenz*)**—agreeing with items out of cognitive ease, politeness, or uncertainty—systematically inflates Likert ratings [cite: 77, 78]. While balanced scales with positively and negatively worded items mitigate distortion in aggregate means (`Acquiescence score = Mean(positive) + Mean(reversed negative) - 2 * scale midpoint`), reverse-worded statements risk confusing 9-year-old readers [cite: 78, 79]. Second, **forced-choice (ipsative) formats** eliminate uniform agreement bias and reduce social desirability faking by forcing choices between statements, yet classical ipsative scoring creates mathematically interdependent trait totals that prevent normative comparison unless modeled with **Thurstonian Item Response Theory (T-IRT)** across sufficient dimensions (simulation studies show intercorrelational bias drops to roughly 0.1 or smaller when 10 or more traits are measured, whereas 5-trait models remain somewhat distorted) [cite: 77, 80, 81, 82, 83].

Third, as **Spörer and Brunstein (2006)** and **Artelt (2000)** emphasize, questionnaire self-reports frequently capture *declarative knowledge* about desirable strategies rather than *actual execution* during learning [cite: 42, 45]. Consequently, the app should replace long surveys with a **hybrid diagnostic architecture**: (1) brief, 1-tap situational check-ins (e.g., 4-point confidence or effort calibration right before and after a task), paired with (2) continuous intra-individual **learning progress diagnostics (*Lernverlaufsdiagnostik*)**, inspired by German evidence-based classroom platforms such as **Lernlinie**, **Levumi**, and **quop** studied by Souvignier and colleagues [cite: 42, 73, 84]. Tracking behavioral process traces—such as whether a child inspects a worked example, manipulates the *Umstellprobe* tool, or completes scheduled spaced reviews—yields higher ecological validity than static self-reports [cite: 42, 48].

### 4.2 Preteen UX Design Principles (Ages 9–12)

Usability research from the Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) on children (90 participants across 53 websites in 2001/2010 studies, synthesized in UX design frameworks for ages 3–12) and on younger teenagers/preteens underscores that ages **9–12** (Grades 4–6) represent a pivotal transition from "learning to read" to "reading to learn" [cite: 40, 91]. Preteens move quickly, make snap judgments, and reject both overly childish, garish graphics and cluttered multimedia animations; yet their reading fluency remains below adult levels, causing them to abandon dense text blocks [cite: 40, 91].

To match this developmental profile, the UI should present content in small, scannable chunks surrounded by generous white space, pair concise text with instructionally purposeful **Dual Coding** visuals rather than decorative mascots, adopt an age-respectful aesthetic suited to aspiring fifth graders, and keep social features opt-in and controlled rather than mandatory [cite: 38, 40].

### 4.3 Autonomy-Supportive Gamification vs. Hyper-Engaging Dark Patterns

German consumer-protection and youth-media regulators—including the **Federation of German Consumer Organisations (vzbv, 2025)**, the **Bavarian Regulatory Authority for Commercial Broadcasting (BLM)**, the **Commission for Youth Media Protection (KJM, 2023)**, and the **BMBFSFJ Expert Commission (2026)**—document widespread deployment of **Hyper-Engaging Dark Patterns (HEDP)** and manipulative *Digital Nudging* aimed at capturing minors' attention through infinite scroll, pull-to-refresh, autoplay, Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) prompts, and aggressive reward loops [cite: 14, 15, 92, 93].

Even well-intentioned German educational platforms illustrate gamification pitfalls: parent guides report that **Antolin's** heavy point accumulation can overshadow intrinsic reading enjoyment and discourage weaker readers [cite: 37], while **ANTON's** coin/trophy loops can induce performance pressure—and parent experience reports note declining enjoyment once school-mandated [cite: 37, 94]. To foster intrinsic motivation while staying strictly clear of HEDPs, the app should eliminate punishing streak-loss mechanics and FOMO countdowns in favor of cumulative mastery milestones; provide finite session endings with explicit offline break prompts rather than infinite queues; report intra-individual strategy growth instead of public class leaderboards; and ban randomized lootboxes or gambling-like reward hooks [cite: 5, 14, 15, 37, 48, 95].

### 5.2 Baden-Württemberg School-IT, MDM, and SCHULE@BW Integration

Institutional adoption in Baden-Württemberg hinges on alignment with the state's school IT ecosystem supported by the *Kultusministerium*, **BITBW**, the **Landesmedienzentrum (LMZ BW)**, **Kreismedienzentren (KMZ)**, and the **State Data Protection Commissioner (LfDI BW)** [cite: 18, 20, 23, 24, 103]. At the platform layer, the state operates **SCHULE@BW** (*Unterricht und Lernen*, *Sichere Kommunikation*, and *Digitaler Arbeitsplatz* via openDesk), offering **Single Sign-On (SSO)** integration for vetted browser-based pedagogical tools—such as the July 2026 onboarding of `minnit'` [cite: 18, 19, 104]. At the device layer, primary schools predominantly deploy **1:N shared iPad carts (*Tablet-Koffer*)** administered through **Apple School Manager (ASM)**, institutional **Volume Purchase Program (VPP)** licenses, and MDM solutions such as **Relution for Education** (hosted locally on **paed.ML** school servers so data remains on-premises) or **Jamf School** supported by Kreismedienzentren [cite: 20, 21, 22, 23, 105, 106].

Reflecting the *Kultusministerium* guidelines (`Hinweise mobile Endgeräte im Unterricht`) and LfDI BW's strict stance against uncontrolled cloud telemetry (as enforced regarding Microsoft 365) [cite: 24, 103], an institutional version of the app must satisfy five technical requirements:
1. **Support Apple "Geteiltes iPad" (*Shared iPad*) and Guest/Temporary Sessions**: Avoid personal Apple IDs and consumer in-app purchases (incompatible with VPP institutional deployment); support persistent Managed Apple ID sessions or temporary guest sessions paired with class-code/QR state recovery [cite: 22, 105].
2. **Encryption and Cloud-Backup Exclusion**: Encrypt locally stored learner data at rest and via HTTPS in transit, and disable automatic consumer cloud backups (e.g., iCloud syncing) so student data never leaves approved storage [cite: 24, 107].
3. **Zero Third-Party Telemetry or Ad SDKs**: Eliminate external analytics SDKs that transmit device fingerprints or behavioral telemetry outside EU or school control [cite: 24, 103, 107].
4. **Pseudonymous / Anonymous Classroom Mode**: Allow teachers to provision alias-based slots (e.g., `Mogli56`), avoiding EU-GDPR/LDSG triggers altogether when no personal or personally identifiable data are processed [cite: 24].
5. **Pre-Drafted AVV & SCHULE@BW Readiness**: Provide EU/German hosting, an LfDI-compatible **AVV** template, automatic end-of-school-year data deletion workflows, and web/PWA readiness for **SCHULE@BW** SSO [cite: 19, 20, 24].

### 6.2 Open Educational Resources (OER) Integration

Integrating vetted **Open Educational Resources (OER)** reduces content-authoring overhead and strengthens institutional credibility in Baden-Württemberg. Specifically, the **Landesbildungsserver Baden-Württemberg (`schule-bw.de`)** records 2.2 million monthly page views and publishes extensive BW-aligned materials under **CC BY 4.0**, permitting legal adaptation with attribution [cite: 115, 116]. Nationally, **WirLernenOnline (WLO)** indexes 283,583 resources—including 25,019 editorially and legally vetted materials across 27 subject portals—and provides open APIs already integrated with state school clouds [cite: 117]. Complementarily, **ZUM-Unterrichten** and **edulabs** offer teacher-vetted **CC BY / CC BY-SA** resources (filtered by digital KMK competencies on *edulabs*), while **H5P**, **LearningApps.org**, **Openverse**, and the **Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek (DDB)** supply open interactive task formats and CC-licensed media [cite: 118].

### 6.3 Synthesized App Architecture and Product Roadmap

Translating this cross-disciplinary evidence into a Grade 4 application for Baden-Württemberg yields seven actionable engineering and product specifications:

1. **Eliminate Learning-Style Sorting; Deploy Content-Matched Dual Coding**: Omit VARK or Vester onboarding quizzes [cite: 1, 2, 4]. Include a brief parent onboarding note explaining why combined visual-verbal Dual Coding and active recall outperform fixed-style labeling [cite: 3, 38].
2. **Build a Baden-Württemberg Grade 4 Curriculum Engine**: Anchor core practice in **Deutsch V2** (evidence-highlighting reading comprehension, *Grundwortschatz BW* spelling strategies, interactive *Satzglieder/Umstellprobe*) and **Mathematik V2** (written arithmetic and *Päckchen*, 3D *Würfelgebäude/Würfelnetze*, *Daten/Zufall* tree diagrams) [cite: 10, 11, 39, 57], structured across *grundlegend (G)*, *mittel (M)*, and *erweitert (E)* tiers to build calm readiness for **Kompass 4** and the **Potenzialtest** [cite: 39, 49].
3. **Embed a 3-Step SRL Coaching Loop (*Planen – Üben – Prüfen*)**: Frame every 10- to 15-minute mission around Zimmerman and Dignath/Stoeger principles [cite: 5, 48]: realistic micro-goal choice, guided practice with worked examples and fading hints, and brief confidence calibration plus constructive error reflection.
4. **Use Hybrid Behavioral Telemetry Instead of Long Surveys**: Exclude 80–90 item Likert inventories [cite: 70, 73]. Combine 1-tap micro-check-ins with process analytics (retrieval accuracy, spacing intervals, hint reliance, *Umstellprobe* traces) modeled on German *Lernverlaufsdiagnostik* platforms [cite: 42, 78, 84].
5. **Enforce Preteen UX and Zero-Dark-Pattern Gamification**: Use an age-respectful, uncluttered layout with chunked text [cite: 40]. Strictly prohibit FOMO timers, streak punishments, lootboxes, public leaderboards, and infinite-scroll queues, replacing them with cumulative mastery milestones and explicit session-end cues [cite: 14, 15, 37, 86].
6. **Engineer Dual-Track Legal Compliance (B2C + B2B)**: Host exclusively in the EU with zero ads and zero profiling (`DSA Art. 28`), gate all billing and parental consent behind a verified parent area (`DSGVO Art. 8`, `UWG`), write privacy notices in child-friendly German, and maintain a closed content perimeter without open links or unmoderated chat (`JMStV`) [cite: 16, 95, 97].
7. **Ship Baden-Württemberg School-IT Compatibility from Day One**: Provide an account-free or pseudonymous classroom mode (`Mogli56`-style aliases) with local-backup opt-out, full support for **Apple School Manager VPP / Geteiltes iPad** (Relution/Jamf School), an LfDI-ready **AVV**, and web/PWA readiness for **SCHULE@BW** Single Sign-On [cite: 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24].
